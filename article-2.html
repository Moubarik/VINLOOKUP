VIN Basics Every Buyer Should Know

The vehicle identification number is a 17-character code required on modern U.S. road vehicles. NHTSA's public decoder can pull manufacturer and plant data directly from those characters. A vehicle history report goes further by linking the same VIN to later events: title transfers, salvage declarations, mileage filings, theft reports, registrations, and accident claims.

Check the windshield plate, door jamb, title, registration, and insurance documents. Mismatched VINs are a hard stop.

Cost Reality of Paid Reports

One CARFAX report is currently $49.99; packages scale to $119.99 for four. AutoCheck starts at $29.99 and offers a five-report package at $59.99. Running five individual paid reports can exceed $150–$250. Free VIN lookup tools let you reject the worst candidates first so you only pay for the cars still in contention.

Signals a Free VIN Check Can Surface

Title brands (salvage, rebuilt, flood, junk, lemon), accident or total-loss flags, odometer sequences that decline, NICB stolen-vehicle and insurance-salvage hits, NHTSA unrepaired recalls, ownership count estimates, and multi-state registration patterns that may indicate flood-region exposure.

Recommended Research Order

1. Locate and verify the physical VIN against paperwork. 2. Run a free VIN lookup. 3. Pull NHTSA recall status. 4. Run NICB VINCheck for theft and salvage. 5. Order an NMVTIS or commercial report only for remaining serious candidates. 6. Get a pre-purchase inspection.

FAQ Snapshot

Yes, free VIN tools exist. No, they are not identical to Carfax. Some require no sign-up. A clean free result does not prove the car has never been damaged or stolen. Always finish with a physical inspection.

Run the free VIN lookup first. Keep the paid report and the mechanic for the car you actually intend to buy.
